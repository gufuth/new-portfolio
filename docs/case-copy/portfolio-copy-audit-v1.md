# ianrluna.com — Full Copy Audit & Rebuild Plan

**Date:** 2026-07-06 · **Scope:** all 17 pages, copy and case-study structure only (not visual design) · **Lens:** hiring CDs, ECDs, and creative recruiters **Method:** every page fetched and extracted verbatim (site_copy_extraction_v1.md in this folder), plus researched hiring-side criteria (sources at bottom).

## 1. The verdict

The raw material is genuinely strong: a real A-list client roster (MrBeast/Amazon, Virgin Galactic, Nike SB, Cuervo, The Atlantic), two or three case studies with real narrative arcs, and a personality register (the fake testimonials, Citizen Kane, "Eat it, Dad") that most books would kill for.

What's holding it back is not the work. It's three copy-level problems:

1. **Production hygiene defects** that a hiring CD reads as "doesn't check his own work." Instant credibility damage, fully fixable in a day.
2. **Templated case-study prose** built on constructions that, in 2026, read as machine-written. For a copywriter, this is the single most dangerous finding in the audit.
3. **Metrics that read as inflated because they're unattributed**, sitting next to case studies with no numbers at all.

Fix these three and the book moves from "solid mid-senior" to "call him in."

## 2. The bar: what hiring creatives actually screen for

Compressed from current recruiter and CD guidance (full sources at bottom):

- **The 3-second scan is real.** Recruiters decide whether your thinking is worth their time in the first glance. The homepage and the first case study carry disproportionate weight.
- **4–8 deep case studies beat 14 thumbnails.** Portfolio educators aiming at CD-level books recommend 5–6 tightly curated campaigns; more than 8 rarely gets read.
- **The universal case anatomy:** Context → Challenge → Idea → Results, one screen per project, scannable in under two minutes. Reviewers explicitly look for "ability to simplify complex problems and nail a brief."
- **Role clarity is a screening criterion.** "Led the campaign" with no specifics is a named red flag. They want to know exactly what YOU did versus the team, stated without ego and without vagueness.
- **Metrics need attribution language.** Unsourced business numbers ("$50M in revenue") are flagged as a credibility red flag. The credible pattern: state the number, the source, the timeframe, and your lane ("results from client analytics; my role: lead copy from concept through production").
- **What separates hired from competent:** a distinct point of view, evidence of leadership (for ACD/CD roles), voice range, and at least one self-initiated project that shows what you make when nobody briefs you.

## 4. Finding two — the copy formula problem (the big one)

The case-study prose runs on a small set of recycled constructions:

- **"It wasn't just X; it was Y"** — appears on 7+ pages ("wasn't just another bottle on the shelf; it was a statement," "wasn't just about watching a launch," "wasn't just adding a new vehicle," "wasn't just seen; it was talked about," "didn't just drop in 2005," "didn't just evolve," "didn't just show up, it set the standard")
- **"The result?"** rhetorical pivot — 6 pages
- **"The Math is in the Pudding"** — reused verbatim as a header on two different case studies
- Plus: "seamlessly integrating" (MrBeast page), "impactful campaigns" and "results-driven solutions" (resume)

Here is why this is now lethal, and it wasn't in 2022: **the negation-pair construction ("it's not just X, it's Y") and the "The result?" pivot have become the most widely recognized tells of AI-generated copy.** Hiring CDs in 2026 are primed to spot them, and many use them as an explicit screen. When a copywriter's own book leans on these patterns, the reviewer draws one of two conclusions, and both end the conversation: the book was written by a machine, or the writer's natural register is indistinguishable from one.

The brutal irony: **the proof that Ian can fix this is already on the site.** The best writing in the book is the writing that sounds like nobody else:

- "First, I learned who MrBeast was."
- "Don Draper never had to do any of this."
- "Eat it, Dad."
- "Five stars." — Ex girlfriend
- The entire Citizen Kane page.

That deadpan, specific, self-aware register is the hire-me voice. The rebuild rule is simple: **write every case study in the voice of the jokes.** If a sentence could appear in anyone else's book, cut it or rewrite it until it couldn't.

## 5. Finding three — case anatomy is inconsistent (the template)

Right now: only Outdoor Voices states a real, specific business problem. Only 5 of 11 case pages carry any results. ROLES credit lines appear on 6 pages in two different formats and are missing from the two flagship stories (MrBeast and Nike). Almost no page names its central idea as an idea.

And one structural gap that matters more for a copywriter than anything else: **almost no page shows the actual copy Ian wrote.** The OV captions are the only place the reader sees real lines. A copywriter's case study that doesn't quote the copy is a chef's menu with no food.

**The standard case-study spec (every page, same skeleton, 250–350 words):**

1. **Hook line** — one line, in the voice. (The MrBeast page's "Giving away millions of dollars? Pfft, that's easy." already does this.)
2. **Context strip** — one line: client, year, agency, your title. ("Virgin Galactic · 2021 · WITHIN · Team lead, copy & TOV")
3. **The problem** — 2–3 sentences, in business terms. What was broken or at stake, with a specific detail that proves you understood it.
4. **The idea, named** — one bolded line. If the campaign had a name ("Find Your Unforgettable," "Let's Play," "Boldly/Together"), lead with it and explain it in one sentence. If it didn't, name the mechanic.
5. **The work + the lines** — assets, and 2–4 actual lines you wrote, labeled as such. This is the section that currently doesn't exist anywhere.
6. **Results** — 2–4 numbers max, each with source and timeframe (see Finding four).
7. **Credit line** — one standard format sitewide: ROLE: Lead copy, TOV development · TEAM: [CD], [AD], [agency]. Crediting the team reads as leadership, not weakness; recruiters explicitly flag singular credit-claiming as a red flag.
8. **One-line takeaway** — what this case proves about you. ("This is what I do with a brand voice that's gone scattered.")

## 6. Finding four — the metrics problem, and feasible numbers per project

**The rule first, because it protects you:** never invent a number. Recruiters name "vague, unverified business impact claims" as a top rejection trigger, and the industry-standard fix is attribution language. Three tiers of credible proof, in order of strength:

- **Tier A — publicly verifiable facts.** Press coverage, App Store rank charts, StockX resale data, platform-reported viewership, company announcements. Strongest, because a skeptical CD can check.
- **Tier B — platform/client analytics you can retrieve or request.** Follower deltas, engagement rates, view counts, CTRs from the period. Cite as "(platform analytics, [period])" or "(client-reported)."
- **Tier C — honest qualitative outcomes.** Renewals, adoption, awards, client quotes. "The client renewed the campaign four times over 18 months" is a result, and it's bulletproof.

The attribution sentence that makes big numbers safe: *"Results below are campaign-level, from [client analytics / press / platform data]. My lane: lead copy from concept through production."* One line like this per case page converts every number from a red flag into evidence you understand how attribution works.

## 7. Finding five — what employers expect that's missing

1. **A homepage positioning line.** The homepage currently has zero copy: no name, no title, no claim. The 3-second scan lands on tile labels. Add one line above the grid: who you are, what you make, one flash of the voice.
2. **Real testimonials next to the fake ones.** The joke page is great, and it lands twice as hard with three real quotes beside it (a CD at WITHIN, a client, a collaborator). Social proof is a named screening criterion, and its total absence is conspicuous. Keep "Five stars. — Ex girlfriend" as the closer.
3. **Role clarity for the ACD → CD jump.** If the target is CD roles, the book must show leadership evidence: team size led, pitches won (the Cuervo ROLES line mentions "Pitching (and winning)": expand that into a sentence somewhere), mentoring, the SCOOBA adoption story. Consider a short "How I lead" section on the Who page replacing the boilerplate middle paragraph ("pushing boundaries, challenging the status quo, indelible mark, color outside the lines" — these are the exact clichés the rest of the site makes fun of, and they're the weakest 60 words on the site).
4. **An AI-era signal.** 2025–26 senior creative job specs increasingly ask about AI-assisted workflow fluency. You have an unusual, true story here (you built your own AI-assisted creative ops system). One paragraph or one self-initiated case study about how you use it while keeping taste and judgment human. Zero competitors' books have this; it reads as senior, current, and unafraid.
5. **A resume that sounds like the site.** The resume is pure LinkedIn-ese and clashes with everything around it. Its metrics also quietly conflict with the case pages (e.g., "35% QoQ social engagement jump" for Virgin Galactic vs. the VG page's numbers). Rewrite in the site's register, reconcile every number with the case studies, fix the dates.
6. **Navigation and metadata.** Label the LinkedIn link. Fill every page title and meta description (recruiters Google you; "Gallery 3 — Ian's Book" is what they currently see). Add a next-case link and a contact line at the bottom of every case page so the scan never dead-ends.
